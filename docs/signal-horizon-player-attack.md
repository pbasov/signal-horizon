# SIGNAL HORIZON — A Player's Attack on the GDD

### v0.1 · companion to GDD v0.8.1 · read-only design critique

> **What this is.** An attack on the Game Design Document written from the *player's* point of view. It walks the entire GDD (§0 through §10 and the version footer), challenges the claims most prone to confirmation bias, reconciles the designer's view with the player's view, verifies claims against the precedents the GDD itself cites and against established game-design theory, and proposes concrete adjustments. It does **not** modify the GDD (the GDD is the user's domain). It does **not** run playtests — the user handles empirical validation. It is a hypothesis-pressure-test: the strongest case a player-advocate can mount *before* a human ever sits down.
>
> **Its relationship to the prior critique.** A separate adversarial review (`FINDINGS.md`-adjacent work) attacked the *project's execution* (building past an unrun gate). This document attacks the *design's claims about the player's experience*. The spine of both is the same finding, restated from the player's seat: **the GDD is a designer's document, and the player never reads it.** Every claim below is tested against that asymmetry.
>
> **Method.** For each GDD section: (1) state the claim as the player would encounter it, (2) challenge the confirmation-bias-prone framing, (3) verify against cited precedent or design theory where possible, (4) propose an adjustment. Claims marked **[INFERENCE]** are reasoned-by-analogy, not established theory. Claims marked **[THEORY]** rest on named design literature. Everything else is grounded in the GDD's own text or the M1 mechanics spec (`signal-horizon-m1.md`).
>
> **Scope fences honoured.** This document designs and writes only. It does not propose that the author run playtests, build code, or edit the GDD. Adjustments are design-level proposals for the user to weigh.

## 0. The Meta-Attack: the GDD is a designer's document, and that is the load-bearing confirmation-bias risk

Before walking the sections, the single finding that organises everything else — the one the prior critique named as #10 and that the objective singles out.

**The claim (implicit, structural).** The GDD describes the fun the player *will have*. It does so in a vocabulary — "taming the sprawl," "optimising against the parse," "the same verb at two bars," "the escalation engine," "the leverage curve" — that is a *designer's* vocabulary. None of these phrases will occur to a player. A player says "it was a mess and now it works," or "I got it working and then I wanted it faster," or "this is annoying," or nothing at all and closes the tab.

**The confirmation-bias mechanism.** The GDD is unusually good at defending its choices, and it cites precedents for nearly every one. But every precedent is cited *in favour* of a design decision. *Cities: Skylines II* is cited as the cautionary tale for invisible sims; *Universal Paperclips* and *Frostpunk* are cited to de-risk the currency flip; *KSP* is cited for the fidelity-where-fun rule; *OpenTTD* for the escalation engine; *Mini Metro* for the visualisation standard; *Stellaris* for the opt-in victory path; *EVE* for the brokering warning; *Zachtronics* for the optimisation pleasure; *Per Aspera* for the datacentre caution; *Duskers*/*Uplink* for the aesthetic. Not once is a precedent cited *against* a choice the GDD went on to make anyway. That is the textbook signature of confirmation bias: a research engine pointed only at support. The playbook the user supplied names this directly — *"Ask an AI tool for evidence supporting what you already believe, and it will find it."*

**The deeper structural risk.** A document this self-consistent creates a *local optimum*: every section reinforces every other, so the document feels airtight, and airtightness is mistaken for correctness. But the player's experience is not generated by the document's internal consistency. It is generated by the *mechanics* (MDA's M), mediated by the *dynamics* (MDA's D), producing the *aesthetics* (MDA's A) — and the inference from M to A is the hardest step in game design, the one most prone to designer-side confirmation bias. **[THEORY]** The MDA framework (Hunicke, LeBlanc, Zubek 2004) is explicit that designers work mechanically *upward* toward aesthetics while players experience aesthetics *downward* toward mechanics, and that the two directions do not guarantee each other. The GDD's confident mapping of "this mechanic ⇒ this player feeling" is exactly the mapping MDA warns is non-trivial.

**The reconciliation.** The designer's view ("the design is coherent and self-aware") and the player's view ("I have not been asked, and the document that claims to know my feelings has never met me") are *both true*, and they do not contradict. A coherent hypothesis is not a validated theory. The GDD is a strong hypothesis. The player-POV attack is the experiment design that would falsify or confirm it. The rest of this document is that experiment design, section by section.

**Adjustment 0 (meta).** Adopt, as a standing discipline, the rule the GDD applies to the physics but not yet to its own claims: *the simulation never lies, but the design document can.* Every player-experience claim in the GDD should be tagged as **[CLAIMED]** until a playtest confirms it, at which point it becomes **[OBSERVED]**. The GDD currently presents all such claims as established. Tagging them would make the confirmation-bias surface visible without rewriting the document.

## 1. §1 Vision Statement — "information is the product" is a designer's framing, not a player's motivation

**The claim.** "You are not a rocket company. You are an information empire." The product sold is "knowledge moved across distance." The signature tension is "physics versus economics."

**The challenge.** A player does not sit down to "move knowledge across distance." A player sits down to *build something that grows, make numbers go up, and feel smart doing it.* The vision statement is a *theme*, and themes do not motivate play; *mechanics* motivate play. **[THEORY]** Raph Koster's *Theory of Fun* (2005) argues the fun is in *learning the underlying pattern*, not in the theme draped over it; a player who finds the pattern boring will not be saved by the theme, and a player who finds it compelling will be compelled regardless of theme. The GDD's vision is evocative for a *pitch deck* (the one-line pitch is good), but it is risk-neutral as a *player motivator* — it tells the player what the game is *about*, not why they should *care*.

**Verification against precedent.** *Factorio*'s vision is "build a factory and launch a rocket" — banal as theme, but the *mechanic* (belts, throughputs, bottlenecks) is the hook. *DSP*'s vision ("build a Dyson Sphere") is grandiose, but again the hook is the logistics. *OpenTTD* has almost no vision statement at all; the hook is "trains make money, more trains make more money." The precedents suggest the vision statement's job is to set tone, not to carry motivation — and Signal Horizon's vision does the tone job well. The risk is treating it as *also* doing the motivation job, which the precedents do not support.

**The player's question.** "When I place my first satellite and a region lights up, am I feeling *'I am an information empire'* or am I feeling *'I made a thing connect and it paid me'*?" The GDD assumes the former elevates the latter. It may not. The latter is the reliable hook; the former is garnish.

**Adjustment 1.** Treat the vision as tone, not as a load-bearing motivator. The M1 onboarding script (Part IV) already does this correctly — Act 1's hook is "launch → cover → paid," which is the mechanic-as-motivator. The risk is in the *GDD's framing* of the vision as if it does motivational work it empirically cannot. No design change needed; a framing correction in how the vision is weighted against the mechanics.

## 2. §2 Design Pillars — seven pillars is five too many for a player to feel, and the "make-or-break" pillar hides a second make-or-break pillar

**The claim.** Seven pillars: information is the product; physics non-negotiable but only where fun; distance creates strategy; the invisible made visible (THE make-or-break); scales gracefully outward; success creates the next problem; leverage compounds.

**The challenge (confirmation bias).** Pillar 4 ("the invisible made visible") is named *the* make-or-break pillar. But there is a second, unnamed make-or-break pillar hiding in the list: Pillar 6 ("success creates the next problem — the escalation engine"). The escalation engine is what makes the game a *game* and not a *puzzle* — a puzzle is solved once, a game sustains. If the escalation engine fails, the game is a one-shot optimisation toy, which is exactly the "finishes-and-shrugs" failure mode the M1 gate tests for. By naming only Pillar 4 as make-or-break, the GDD directs disproportionate attention to visualisation (which is genuinely solved in the build) and under-weights escalation (which is, per the backlog, authored via a scenario beat table — the weak way the GDD's own §3b rejects).

**The challenge (pillar count).** Seven pillars is a designer's scaffolding, not a player's experience. **[INFERENCE]** In design practice, a "pillar" is a heuristic the *team* uses to resolve disputes ("does this feature serve a pillar?"), not a thing the player perceives. Players perceive *one* dominant aesthetic at a time. Citing seven pillars risks treating all seven as co-equal design pressures, which produces feature accretion (each pillar "deserves" systems) — the scope-creep failure the GDD's own Risk 1 names as existential.

**Verification.** The GDD cites *Mini Metro* for the visualisation standard. *Mini Metro* has, effectively, *one* pillar (legibility) and executes it ruthlessly, cutting everything that doesn't serve it. Signal Horizon citing *Mini Metro* for one pillar while carrying seven is a selective read of the precedent. *Mini Metro*'s discipline *is* the lesson, not just its visualisation technique.

**Adjustment 2.** (a) Promote the escalation engine to co-make-or-break status with visualisation. Both must be proven at M1; neither can be assumed. (b) Distinguish "design pillars" (team heuristics, all seven fine) from "player-felt pillars" (at most two: *I can see it working* and *my success makes it harder*). The M1 gate should test the two player-felt pillars explicitly, not the seven design pillars.

## 3. §3 Core Loop & §3a "What the Fun Is" — the fun is named precisely, and that precision is the confirmation-bias trap

**The claim.** §3a names the fun: the spine is *taming the sprawl* (chaos → order, measured against "does it work?"); the mastery layer is *optimising against the parse* (order → optimal, measured against "how close to theoretical best?"); they are the same verb at two bars, hinged on a legible record.

**The challenge (confirmation bias — the central one).** §3a is the most sophisticated and the most dangerous section of the GDD. Its danger is that it *names the fun so well* that it becomes unfalsifiable. "Taming the sprawl" is a feeling; "optimising against the parse" is a feeling; the GDD asserts they are the same verb at two bars, and that the hinge is a legible record. But none of this has been tested against a player, and the assertion has a convenient escape hatch: if a tester doesn't report the optimisation pull, the design can say "they didn't open the parse" (the novice "never opens it and tames happily to functional"). If a tester *does* report it, the design is confirmed. This is not falsifiable as stated — the theory absorbs both outcomes.

**Verification against the cited precedents.** §3a cites *WoW* combat logs and *Zachtronics* histograms. Both precedents cut both ways:
- *WoW*: the combat log/parse culture emerged *from the player community*, not from Blizzard's design. Blizzard shipped the log; players invented "the parse" as a competitive sport. **[INFERENCE]** This suggests the optimisation pleasure is *player-constructed*, not designer-delivered. A designer who builds "the parse" and expects players to optimise against it may be inverting the causality: players optimise against records they *chose* to keep, and the game's job is to make the record *available*, not to *prescribe* what optimising looks like.
- *Zachtronics*: the histogram works because the puzzles are *bounded* (you solved it; here's your cost vs. everyone's). Signal Horizon's network is *unbounded* (there is no "solved," only "better"). The Zachtronics pleasure depends on a discrete solution boundary; Signal Horizon has a continuous one. **[INFERENCE]** The transfer may not hold: optimising an unbounded system against a theoretical optimum is a different pleasure (closer to *Factorio* megabase ratios than to *Zachtronics* histograms), and the GDD cites the wrong precedent.

**The reconciliation.** The designer's view: "I have named the fun precisely, so I can build for it." The player's view: "I will either feel a pull to redo it better, or I won't — and the designer cannot know which until I play." The reconciliation: §3a's precision is *valuable as a build target* and *worthless as a prediction*. The GDD treats it as both. The correction is to treat §3a as a hypothesis with a specific falsifiable form: *after a one-hour session, ≥40% of cold testers spontaneously express a desire to redo a specific decision.* (The 40% threshold borrows from the Sean Ellis PMF test the playbook cites, adapted to a single-session gate.)

**Adjustment 3.** Make the §3a claim falsifiable and measured. The M1 gate already asks "does the tester finish wanting to do it better?" — sharpen this to a *behavioural* measure: do they, *unprompted*, name a specific thing they would do differently? "Satisfied-and-done" is a fail; "I'd do X differently" is a pass; "I'd do X, Y, and Z differently and here's why" is a strong pass. Record the *specificity* of the stated improvement, not just its presence. This converts the §3a claim from unfalsifiable to measurable.

## 4. §3b The Escalation Engine — the engine is endogenous in the sim and authored in the experience, and the GDD conflates the two

**The claim.** Three endogenous generators (demand grows where served; freshness decays; automation is outgrown) plus an across-tier rule (each tier invalidates a prior strategy). The weak way (designer places it) is rejected; the strong way (success degrades position) is the design.

**The challenge (confirmation bias).** The GDD says the *experience* of escalation must be endogenous ("the next gap is self-generated"). But the M1 spec (Part I §9, Part IV) delivers escalation *via a scenario beat table* that opens Act 3a only when Act 2's gate fires, gated on a state predicate. The systems run for real; the *arrival* is scripted. The GDD's §3b rejects "the designer places it" — and then the M1 spec places it, by gating `escalationOn` behind a cursor beat.

This is the same finding as the prior critique's #6, restated from the player's seat: the player will *experience* escalation as "the game decided I'm ready for more," not as "my success caused this." The GDD's claim is about *felt causality*, and felt causality is broken by the state gate. A tester who says "my success caused the congestion" is *wrong* about the mechanism; the beat table caused it. The GDD's §3b is a claim about player *attribution*, and the design's own state-gating undermines the attribution it needs.

**Verification against *OpenTTD*.** *OpenTTD*'s escalation *is* genuinely endogenous in experience: a profitable route attracts more cargo, more cargo needs more trains, more trains congest the junction. The player *sees* the cargo queue grow because they served it. Signal Horizon's M1 escalation grows `offeredLoad` on a served contract — which *is* endogenous in the sim — but the player sees it *only after the beat fires*, and the beat fires on a state check the player didn't trigger. *OpenTTD* has no state gate; Signal Horizon does. The precedent the GDD cites does not support the gated delivery.

**The reconciliation.** The engine is real; the *reveal* is scheduled. The player's view: "I was doing fine, then it got harder — was that me or the game?" The designer's view: "the systems are real, only the order is scripted." Both are true, and the player cannot tell them apart — which is the problem. The player attributes the escalation to whichever is more salient, and a state-gated reveal is more salient than a closed-form load growth.

**Adjustment 4.** Decouple the *engine* from the *reveal* in at least one M1 variant. Run a playtest condition where escalation is *on from the start* (no `act3a.emit` gate) and the scenario only controls *contract arrival*, not the escalation flag. If testers in that condition report the escalation as "my success caused it" more often than testers in the gated condition, the gating is the attribution breaker and should be dropped. This is a playtest-design proposal for the user, not a code change.

## 5. §4.1 Orbital Mechanics & Sim/Render Split — the honesty claim is about the sim, but the player meets the *lie*, and the lie is under-examined

**The claim.** The sim is f64 truth; the render is f32 lie. The sim never lies to make the player comfortable. "Fidelity in the dimension that's fun; abstraction everywhere it's a chore."

**The challenge.** The honesty claim is about the *simulation*. The player never meets the simulation; the player meets the *rendered lie*. The GDD says the lie is "visual cheating" (log-compression, floating-origin, icon collapse) and that this is fine because the *decisions* are computed in truth. But the M1 spec (§3.2, the planner design rule) extends the "truth" to the *planner preview* — the player sees a truthful footprint/latency preview before commit. So the player is, in fact, meeting truth-via-preview. Good. But the GDD's honesty claim is broader than the M1 spec's honesty delivery: the GDD claims the *whole game* is honest, while the M1 spec is carefully honest only about *the planner preview and the link budget*. The gap between the two is unexamined.

**Specifically:** the M1 toy scales (`A1_BODY_RADIUS_M=300_000`, `GEO_PERIOD_S=240`, `LEO_PERIOD_S=150`) are *lies* — a 300km Earth radius, a 4-minute GEO period. The GDD's §4.1 says "real SI units (metres, seconds, kilograms)" and the M1 spec says "toy scales are explicit Act-1 toys; the *ratio* is the faithful part." The player does not know the ratio is the faithful part; the player sees a 300km Earth and a 4-minute orbit and may conclude the game is *not* honest, which is the *opposite* of the GDD's intent. The honesty claim is internally coherent (the ratio is preserved) but *communicatively broken* (the player sees the toy and has no reason to infer the ratio-fidelity).

**Verification against *KSP*.** *KSP* is cited for "fidelity where fun, abstraction where chore." But *KSP*'s solar system is *scaled down* (Kerbin is 600km radius vs Earth's 6371km) and *KSP* players *know* this and accept it as a gameplay concession. The precedent supports toy scales *if the player is told*. Signal Horizon's M1 does not tell the player the Earth is a toy. The precedent is cited for the fidelity principle but not for the communication principle that makes the toy acceptable.

**Adjustment 5.** Either (a) surface the toy-scale honesty to the player (a SYSTEM.LOG line on first launch noting the Earth is rendered at a compressed scale for legibility, preserving orbital ratios — diegetic, not a tutorial popup), or (b) make the honesty claim in the GDD scope-limited to "the *physics decisions* are honest; the *visual scales* are gameplay concessions," so the claim matches what the player actually encounters. The GDD currently over-claims; the M1 spec under-claims; they should meet in the middle.

## 6. §4.2 Information & Coverage — "multi-axis scoring is the optimisation spine" is asserted, but the M1 floor hides the axes, so the spine is invisible at the floor

**The claim.** Five information dimensions (connectivity, bandwidth, latency, observation, freshness); multi-axis scoring is "the optimisation spine, not a footnote"; a solution is rated on competing axes, so there is no single right build.

**The challenge.** The M1 spec (§4.4, §II.2.2) deliberately *hides* the axes in Act 1 (`activeAxes={connectivity}` only) and introduces them one at a time across acts. This is correct pedagogy (one concept per act). But it means the "multi-axis scoring" the GDD calls the spine is *not present at the floor*. The floor is single-axis (connectivity only). The spine appears only in Act 3, and even then "one at a time." The GDD's §3a constraint — "the floor verb and ceiling verb must be the same verb" — is *violated by the scoring system itself*: the floor verb is "satisfy connectivity," the ceiling verb is "trade off four axes," and these are not the same verb at different altitudes; they are different verbs.

**Verification against *Factorio* ratios.** *Factorio*'s optimisation spine (throughput, power, pollution, space) is present *from the first furnace* — the player is always trading multiple axes, even if unconsciously. Signal Horizon's M1 floor is single-axis by design. The precedent the GDD leans on (multi-axis optimisation as the depth source) does not support a floor that hides all but one axis. **[INFERENCE]** This is the M1 spec's most defensible choice (pedagogy), but it is in tension with the GDD's "spine, not footnote" claim, and the tension is unacknowledged.

**Adjustment 6.** Reconcile the GDD and the M1 spec: the GDD should say "multi-axis scoring is the spine *from M2 onward*; M1 introduces axes one at a time as pedagogy, so the M1 floor is single-axis by design." This is a framing fix, not a mechanic change. It removes the false implication that M1's floor already has the multi-axis spine.

## 7. §4.3a Network Topology & Laser Links — the floor/ceiling "one routing model" claim is strong, but Level 2 is so deferred it cannot be validated, and the "same verb" test is at risk

**The claim.** Level 1 (policy intent + solver) is the floor; Level 2 (physically construct the laser backbone) is the ceiling, available never required, matures at M4. "Level 2 does not bypass Level 1; it feeds it." The floor/ceiling test: the master's version is the novice's version held to a higher bar.

**The challenge.** Level 2 is deferred to M4 and gets "a placement marker, not a detailed design, until the core loop is proven fun" (per the GDD's own discipline). So the floor/ceiling "same verb" claim for the topology system is *unverifiable until M4*, and is being made *now*, before M1. This is a claim about a system that does not exist, validated against a gate that has not run. The confirmation-bias risk: the GDD is using the *future* Level 2 to justify the *present* Level 1's depth ("don't worry, the ceiling is there"), when the ceiling is a marker in a doc.

**The "same verb" test applied honestly.** The novice verb is "accept a contract, let the solver route, watch." The master verb (Level 2) is "choose which backbone nodes peer, spend scarce terminals, design a topology that survives the orbital cycle." Are these the same verb at different altitudes? **[INFERENCE]** They are not. One is *consumption of a solver's output*; the other is *authorship of a graph*. The GDD's claim that "a Level-2 player is still playing Level 1; they've just improved the substrate" is a designer's assertion, not a player's experience. A player who goes from "watch the solver route" to "design a cycle-robust laser mesh" has changed verbs.

**Verification.** The GDD cites *Kubernetes* (declarative intent, the system converges). But *Kubernetes* operators *are* doing a different verb than `ssh`-ing into a box — the GDD's own analogy concedes the verb changes. The "unit of command rises" framing (§4.11) is honest about this *for the leverage curve*, but §4.3a's "one routing model, two depths" framing is not honest about it for the topology system. The two sections are in tension.

**Adjustment 7.** Either (a) concede that Level 2 is a different verb and redesign the bridge so the novice *touches* topology authorship early (even if trivially — e.g., manually pointing one antenna in Act 2, as a seed of "I shape the graph"), or (b) defer the "same verb" claim for the topology system until M4, when it can be tested, and present Level 1 as a complete game in itself (which it is). The GDD currently wants it both ways: Level 2 is promised as the depth, but is also "never required." If it's never required, it's not the ceiling of the same game; it's a different game. Say so.

## 8. §4.4 Light-Speed Delay, Caching & Brokering — the "first thing we prototype" claim is honoured for caching and *dishonoured* for the connectivity game that replaced it

**The claim.** "It is also the first thing we prototype (§9), because if optimising light-delayed information flow isn't fun in a 30-minute slice, nothing downstream saves the game."

**The challenge.** v0.8.1 *corrected* the early game: it is now a "constellation-construction and connectivity puzzle," NOT a caching game. Freshness/caching/light-delay appear in M1 only as a "deliberately-fenced final-act teaser." This correction is *right* (the connectivity game is more legible as a floor), but it creates an unexamined consequence: **the thing the GDD said must be proven first (light-delay optimisation) is now the thing M1 deliberately withholds.** The M1 gate tests the *connectivity* game (Layer 1) and only *teases* the light-delay game (Layer 2, the Mars hook). So the project's founding de-risking question ("is light-delay fun?") has been *deferred*, not answered.

**The confirmation-bias risk.** The GDD treats this as resolved ("the early game is connectivity, not caching"). But the *risk* the original sequencing addressed — "is the signature mechanic fun before we build anything else" — is now answered by a *different* mechanic (connectivity/oversubscription) than the one that carries the campaign (light-delay/caching/freshness). A connectivity game that is fun does *not* imply a light-delay game that is fun. They are different pleasures: connectivity is *spatial* taming (cover the gap, share the link); light-delay is *temporal* taming (pre-stage the cache, predict the blackout). **[INFERENCE]** The M1 gate, as designed, can pass on the spatial pleasure and tell us nothing about the temporal pleasure that is the game's actual signature.

**Verification against *Frostpunk*.** *Frostpunk* is cited for "the rules change but the goal is constant." But *Frostpunk* tests its signature mechanic (the storm, the scarcity) *in the first 20 minutes* — the player meets the core tension immediately. Signal Horizon's M1 defers its signature tension (light-delay) to the final 10 minutes as a teaser. The precedent suggests the signature mechanic should be *front-loaded*, not back-loaded. The GDD cites *Frostpunk* for the currency flip but not for this sequencing lesson.

**The reconciliation.** The designer's view: "connectivity is the honest floor; light-delay is the evolution; you can't teach light-delay before connectivity." The player's view: "you spent an hour teaching me a game and then showed me 10 minutes of a *different* game and asked if I'm hooked." Both are reasonable. The risk is that the M1 gate's Layer 2 (Mars hook) is asked to do too much: validate an entire *campaign premise* on a 10-minute fenced teaser. That is a thin basis for a go/no-go on M2.

**Adjustment 8.** Add a *third* gate layer, or split Layer 2: in addition to "did the Mars teaser hook them," run a *separate* 30-minute light-delay-only playtest (caching/prefetch against a fixed Mars feed, no Earth connectivity) to test the signature mechanic in its own right. The user handles this; the design proposal is to not let the M1 connectivity gate stand in for the light-delay gate. They test different pleasures.

## 9. §4.5 Datacenters & §4.6 Autonomy — "never visibly do something stupid" is specified; "visibly do something smart" is not, and the late game's fun is under-specified

**The claim (§4.6).** Autonomy is diegetic flight software, never "AI." The trust/risk dial is a strategic axis. "The AI must never visibly do something stupid the way CS2's traffic or Per Aspera's drones did."

**The challenge.** The failure mode (visible incompetence) is well-specified. The *success* mode is not. The GDD does not say what *pleasure* the player derives from autonomy that works. The leverage curve (§4.11) says autonomy "absorbs the rote of the tier below" and "the unit of command rises." But "rote absorbed invisibly" is the definition of a system the player stops engaging with. The pleasure of *taming* (§3a) is active; the pleasure of *having tamed* (autonomy running it) is passive, and the GDD has not specified what makes the passive state fun, only what makes it not-annoying.

**Verification against *Factorio* bots and *DSP* logistics.** *Factorio*'s construction bots and *DSP*'s interstellar logistics drones are the precedents for "automation that absorbs rote." Both work because the player *sees* them working (bots fly to build, drones fly to deliver) — the automation is *visible labour*, not invisible competence. **[INFERENCE]** Signal Horizon's autonomy (flight software, policies) is specified as *invisible competence* — the network "just stays up." The precedents suggest the autonomy needs to be *visibly doing work* to be felt as a pleasure, not just as an absence of failure. The GDD's §5 (visualisation) does not list "autonomy working" as one of the three must-be-visible things. It should.

**Adjustment 9.** Add a fourth "must be viscerally visible" item to §5: *autonomy working* — when the player's standing orders keep a distant node alive across a blackout, the player should *see* the autonomy make a decision (a packet re-routed locally, a triage choice logged in SYSTEM.LOG with the reason), not just see the network not break. The pleasure of automation is *watching your agent act on your behalf*, not *noticing nothing went wrong*. This is a §5/§4.6 design addition for the user to consider.

## 10. §4.10 The Information Economy — the four de-risking commitments protect the player's money, not the player's *mastery*

**The claim.** Four commitments: foreshadow from early game; € stays relevant; optional victory path; gradual and legible flip.

**The challenge.** The four commitments all address *retroactive devaluation of investment* (the "bait-and-switch"). They are good. But the research the GDD cites (*Paperclips*, *Frostpunk*) points at a different risk the commitments do not touch: **the flip invalidates the *skill* the player spent hours building.** A player who mastered carrier optimisation (M1–M4) is told, at M6, that the optimisation target has changed (coverage × freshness × uniqueness), and the carrier skill is demoted. € stays useful, but the *mastery* — which §3a says *is* the fun — is no longer the winning skill. The four commitments prevent the *money* from being rug-pulled; they do not prevent the *mastery* from being rug-pulled. And mastery, per §3a, is the fun.

**Verification against *Universal Paperclips*.** *Paperclips* is cited as a beloved currency flip. But *Paperclips*' terminal goal is *constant* ("always maximise paperclips") — the GDD's own §4.10 notes this. *Paperclips* flips the *resource* but never the *goal*. Signal Horizon flips both (the resource from € to information, *and* the win condition from net-worth to information-dominance, even if optional). The precedent the GDD cites to de-risk the flip *does not support flipping the win condition*. The "optional" framing mitigates this (a player can pursue a classic net-worth win), but commitment 4 ("the market reprices over time, visibly") means the *economy* reprices regardless of the player's chosen path — so even a carrier-purist player experiences the skill-devaluation as the market shifts around them.

**The reconciliation.** The designer's view: "the flip is the leverage curve's terminal, felt for hours before it formalises." The player's view: "I got good at X, and now the game rewards Y." Both can be true. The risk is that the player experiences the flip as a *comprehension reset* — the CS2 failure (can't see what the sim is doing) applied to *mastery* rather than to *visualisation*. The GDD de-risks the visualisation side (Pillar 4) but not the mastery side.

**Adjustment 10.** Add a fifth de-risking commitment: **the player's M1–M4 mastery must remain *load-bearing* in the information economy, not be demoted to a prerequisite.** Concretely: the carrier skill (topology, oversubscription, routing) should remain the *substrate* of information dominance (you can't mint information without a backbone that carries it), so the master carrier is *advantaged* entering the flip rather than *reset*. The GDD's §4.10 metabolism ("you must keep feeding the mints") gestures at this but does not commit to it as a de-risking commitment. Make it explicit.

## 11. §4.11 The Leverage Curve — "discovered through operation" is the most elegant and the most untestable claim in the GDD

**The claim.** Capability is discovered through operation, not purchased from a menu. The unit of command rises. The capability-discovery template: operate → hit a wall → investigate in a diagnostic view → recognise the pattern → the tool surfaces there.

**The challenge (confirmation bias — severe).** This is the GDD at its most elegant and its most unfalsifiable. "Discovered through operation" is easy to say and easy to get wrong (the GDD admits this: "spontaneous discovery can't be guaranteed, so for players who don't connect the dots the diagnostic view does more of the work — *nudged* discovery"). But "nudged discovery" is *instruction*, not *discovery*. The §4.11 honest caveat concedes the core claim may not hold, then preserves the framing ("must always look like information that was already there, never an achievement-unlock popup"). This is the same unfalsifiability as §3a: if the player "discovers" it, the design is confirmed; if they don't, the diagnostic "nudged" them, and the design is still confirmed. There is no outcome that falsifies "discovered through operation."

**Verification against *KSP*.** *KSP* is cited as the lineage ("you don't research landing, you learn to land"). But *KSP*'s discovery is *embodied* — the player physically crashes, physically learns to throttle, physically lands. The comprehension is *motor* and *kinesthetic*, not *diagnostic*. Signal Horizon's discovery is *intellectual* — the player reads a trace view, recognises a periodic pattern, infers predictability. **[INFERENCE]** These are different cognitive pleasures. *KSP*'s discovery transfers because it is *physical*; Signal Horizon's discovery may not transfer because it is *read*. The precedent may not support the transferable-knowledge claim for the *discovery mechanism*, only for the *physics content*.

**The sharper point.** The capability-discovery template (operate → wall → diagnose → recognise → tool surfaces) *assumes the player diagnoses.* But diagnosis is a *skill* — the player has to know to open the trace, know to look for periodicity, know to correlate the pattern with geometry. The GDD assumes the diagnostic view *does the comprehension for the player* (the M1-SLV-4 shortfall view names the binding constraint and the kind of fix). If the diagnostic does the comprehension, there is no discovery — there is a tutorial with a detective theme. If the diagnostic *doesn't* do the comprehension, the player may never discover the capability at all (the GDD's admitted failure mode). The design is caught between two failures and has not specified the narrow band between them.

**Adjustment 11.** Make the discovery claim falsifiable: define what "unprompted discovery" looks like behaviourally (the player opens the trace *before* the shortfall fires, or names the periodicity *before* the forecast appears), and measure it at the gate. If ≥1 of 5 testers discovers the predictive-routing concept unprompted, the discovery template has soil. If 0 of 5 do, the diagnostic is doing all the work and the "discovery" framing is a fiction — redesign the surface as honest instruction (which can still be diegetic and elegant) rather than disguised discovery. This is a measurement proposal for the user's playtests.

## 12. §4.12 The Legible Record (The Parse) — the novice "never opens it" is an assumption, and the assumption is the gate's weak point

**The claim.** The parse is "dark matter for the novice and the whole game for the optimiser." A novice tames to functional via live cues and has a complete game. The parse underlies M1 even though its depth is invisible to most players.

**The challenge.** The claim that the novice "has a complete game" without the parse is *the* load-bearing assumption of the floor/ceiling design. If it is wrong — if the novice *needs* the parse to tame to functional — then the floor is not a floor, it is a *wall* with the parse as the only way over. The GDD asserts the novice doesn't need it; the assertion is untested.

**Verification against *Dwarf Fortress* / *Aurora 4X*.** The GDD cites *Aurora 4X* as the "incomprehensible complexity isn't tamable, it's just suffering" failure mode. *Dwarf Fortress* (not cited but adjacent) is the canonical case where the *record* (the game's own logs and reports) is *required* to play at all — novices who don't read the logs cannot tame the fortress. **[INFERENCE]** The precedents suggest that in deep sims, the record is often *not* dark matter for the novice; it is the novice's *only* path to comprehension. Signal Horizon's claim that its live cues are sufficient for the novice is a strong claim against the genre's grain. It may be right (the freshness-as-saturation cue is genuinely brilliant), but it is not supported by the cited precedents — it is asserted *against* them.

**Adjustment 12.** Test the novice-doesn't-need-the-parse claim explicitly at the gate: track whether testers who *do not* open the parse/trace view can reach "functional" (contracts served, strain weathered). If they cannot, the live cues are insufficient and the parse is load-bearing at the floor — which is fine, but means the "dark matter for the novice" framing is wrong and the parse must be surfaced more aggressively, not hidden. Measurement proposal for the user.

## 13. §5 Dashboards & UX — "one excellent view per milestone" is the right discipline; the tiling WM pre-empts it

**The claim.** "One excellent view per milestone, not all nine up front." The three must-be-visible things. The tiling WM.

**The challenge.** The discipline is correct. The *execution* (per the backlog) pre-empts it: the tiling WM, the window-summon rail, the five presets were built in PB.3 and redesigned repeatedly *before* the one excellent view (the orrery-as-dashboard) was validated as fun. The WM is a horizontal layer built before the vertical slice proved it needed a horizontal layer. This is the "agentic technical debt" pattern — building infrastructure ahead of the need — in a specific form: the WM makes it *psychologically easy* to add panels, which makes it easy to add systems, which is the scope-creep failure the GDD's Risk 1 names as existential.

**Verification against *Duskers* / *Uplink*.** Both cited precedents are *single-window* terminal games. Neither has a tiling WM. The GDD cites them for the aesthetic, not for the chrome architecture. **[INFERENCE]** The precedents suggest the 1-bit terminal aesthetic does *not* require a tiling WM to land — a single immersive window is sufficient. The WM is solving a density problem (many panels) that only exists if the game has many panels, which the GDD's own §5 discipline says it shouldn't until late.

**Adjustment 13.** No code change (the WM is built and good). A *discipline* change: freeze the WM's panel count at the M1 gate's needs (orrery + finance + log + trace) and do not add panels until a milestone proves it needs one. The WM's existence is not the problem; the WM's *gravitational pull on scope* is. Name this as a standing risk in §10.

## 14. §6 Technical Architecture — the "no UI framework" decision is well-justified; the iteration-velocity argument is the real pillar, and it is unstated as a *player-facing* benefit

**The claim.** No UI framework; imperative DOM + Three.js; f64 truth / f32 lie; floating origin; determinism first.

**The challenge.** This section is technically strong and I do not contest it. The player-POV observation is narrower: the GDD justifies the stack on *technical* grounds (GC pressure, reconciliation cost, frame budget) and on *iteration velocity* (Vite HMR), but frames iteration velocity as a *developer* benefit. It is also a *player* benefit: faster iteration means the visualisation pillar (the make-or-break) gets more polish passes, which is the thing that makes the invisible visible. The GDD understates this. The stack choice is, indirectly, a player-experience choice, and should be framed as such when the project evaluates whether to keep it.

**Adjustment 14.** No design change. A framing note: when the stack is revisited (e.g., the WebKitGTK question from FINDINGS), the player-facing argument for iteration velocity should be weighed alongside the technical arguments, not after them. The visualisation pillar's quality is downstream of iteration speed.

## 15. §7 Multiplayer — "design now, build maybe-never" is honest; the "thematically perfect" claim is a designer's hope, not a player's draw

**The claim.** Multiplayer is out of scope; the affordance is kept cheap. "The light-delay theme is uniquely suited to multiplayer: information asymmetry between players separated by light-minutes is emergent and thematically perfect."

**The challenge.** The affordance discipline is correct. The "thematically perfect" claim is a designer's aesthetic judgement, not a player-experience prediction. **[INFERENCE]** Multiplayer games succeed on *social* dynamics (rivalry, cooperation, reputation), not on *thematic fit*. *EVE*'s multiplayer works because of the *players*, not because the theme is perfect — the theme is the setting; the social dynamics are the game. Signal Horizon's "information asymmetry across light-minutes" is a *mechanic* for multiplayer, not a *reason to play* multiplayer. A player does not log in for asymmetry; they log in for the people. The GDD conflates thematic suitability with player motivation.

**Adjustment 15.** No change to the multiplayer posture (correct as is). A framing note: if multiplayer is ever pursued, the case for it must be made on *social dynamics* (who would play with whom, and why the asymmetry creates *social* fun, not just *mechanical* fun), not on thematic fit. The "thematically perfect" line should not be used as internal justification to prioritise multiplayer.

## 16. §8 Art & Audio — "monochrome machine, living signal" is the GDD's strongest aesthetic claim and its most defensible; the CVD-safety discipline is exemplary

**The claim.** 1-bit chrome, coloured signal. Freshness-as-saturation. CVD-safe redundant encoding. Monochrome-purist mode.

**The challenge.** This is the section where the player-POV attack has the least to bite. The aesthetic is coherent, the CVD discipline is genuine (redundant encoding, selectable palettes, monochrome-purist as a *playable* mode not a fallback), and the freshness-as-saturation move is the single most legible "invisible made visible" technique in the design. The only player-POV risk: the GDD assumes the freshness-as-saturation cue *reads* without testing it on colour-blind players via the redundant channel. The §8 mitigation (dither/desaturation carries the distinction) is specified, but its *perceptual* adequacy is asserted, not measured.

**Verification.** The CVD discipline matches accessibility best practice (redundant encoding is the WCAG-adjacent standard for colour-as-information). **[THEORY]** Perceptual colour research (e.g., Ware's *Information Visualization*) supports saturation as a preattentive channel *for colour-normal viewers*; for CVD viewers, the dither/shape redundant channel must carry the load, and dither density is a *weaker* preattentive channel than saturation. The risk: dither-as-redundant-encoding may be *legible* (discernible on inspection) but not *preattentive* (instantly readable), which is what "at a glance" requires. The mitigation may be technically compliant but perceptually insufficient.

**Adjustment 16.** Add a gate check: have ≥1 CVD tester play the monochrome-purist mode and report whether freshness reads *at a glance* (preattentive) or *on inspection* (attentive). The distinction matters because §5 demands at-a-glance reading, and dither may only deliver on-inspection. Measurement proposal for the user.

## 17. §9 Development Roadmap — the M1 gate is the project's spine, and it has not run; everything past M1 is, by the GDD's own logic, premature

**The claim.** M1 gates the entire project. "If M1 fails this test, the project pivots or stops." The gate has two layers (Earth sustain + Mars hook).

**The challenge.** This is the finding the prior critique named as #1, restated from the player's seat. The GDD's §9 is *correct*. The problem is that the project (per the backlog) has built M2 (full Earth tycoon slice) and M3a (first datacenter) *before* the M1 gate has run on *any* tester. By the GDD's own logic, M2+ is premature. The player's view: "you built me a sequel before you checked if I liked the first game." The GDD's discipline says don't do this; the project did it anyway.

**The confirmation-bias mechanism.** The GDD is sophisticated enough to make each M2/M3 system *feel* justified by the doc — each has a §4 reference, a precedent, a rationale. So the building *felt* disciplined even as it violated the doc's own sequencing. This is the playbook's "mistaking building for validating" at the project level: a working M2 build feels like evidence the design is right, when it is evidence the *engineering* is right, which is a different claim.

**Adjustment 17.** Run the M1 gate before any further M2+/M3+ work. If the gate fails, the M2/M3 builds are sunk cost (not waste — they taught the engineering — but not evidence the design works). If the gate passes, the M2/M3 builds are validated retroactively. The GDD already says this. The project must do it. This is not a design adjustment; it is an execution discipline the user must enforce. The player-POV attack's strongest recommendation is: *stop building, start testing.*

## 18. §10 Risks & Open Questions — the risk register is honest but weighted toward *design* risks; the *player-experience* risks are under-weighted

**The claim.** Ten risks, re-ranked. Risk 1 (scope) existential; Risk 2 (invisible product) make-or-break; Risk 3 (currency flip) high; etc.

**The challenge.** The risk register is honest about *design* risks (scope, visualisation, currency flip, brokering, datacentres, dead-air, onboarding, aesthetic). It is *thin* on *player-experience* risks that the GDD's own §3a implies but does not list:
- **Risk P1 (absent): the novice floor is not actually a floor.** If the novice needs the parse to tame to functional (Adjustment 12), the floor/ceiling design fails. Not in the register.
- **Risk P2 (absent): the optimisation pull does not fire.** If testers finish satisfied-and-done, the §3a fun is not the fun. The gate tests this, but the *risk* — "what if the signature pleasure doesn't fire" — is not named as a risk; it is named as a gate criterion. Naming it as a risk would force a mitigation design (what do we do if it doesn't fire?) before the gate, not after.
- **Risk P3 (absent): the same-verb test fails at the late game.** If the master's game is a different verb (Adjustment 7), the "easy to pick up, hard to master" framing is a casual toy + expert game stapled on. Not in the register.
- **Risk P4 (absent): the discovery template is unfalsifiable and may be fiction.** If no tester discovers unprompted (Adjustment 11), the leverage curve's legibility mechanism is a tutorial in disguise. Not in the register.

**Adjustment 18.** Add four player-experience risks to §10: P1 (novice floor dependency on the parse), P2 (optimisation pull failure), P3 (same-verb failure at the ceiling), P4 (discovery template unfalsifiability). Each should have a mitigation that is a *playtest measurement*, not a design assertion. This re-weights the register from "what can go wrong with the design" to "what can go wrong with the *player's experience* of the design," which is the thing the gate actually tests.

## 19. Version footer & §0 change-log — the document's own evolution reveals a pattern: each revision *adds* sophistication, none *removes* a claim that playtesting refuted

**The claim (implicit).** The GDD is a living document; git history tracks revisions; each version improved the design.

**The challenge.** Reading §0 and the footer across versions v0.3 → v0.8.1, a pattern emerges: every revision *adds* (datacentres, autonomy, information economy, colour, topology, leverage, the parse, oversubscription, capability-discovery). No revision *removes* a player-experience claim that was later refuted by playtesting — because there has been no playtesting to refute anything. The document has only ever grown more sophisticated in one direction (more design depth, more precedent citations, more self-aware risk naming). A document that has only ever been revised by its author, against no external signal, is a document whose revisions track the *author's increasing confidence*, not the *design's increasing validation*. **[INFERENCE]** This is the structural signature of a design that has been *iterated in the absence of feedback*, which is the exact condition confirmation bias thrives in.

**The reconciliation.** This is not a criticism of the revisions — each was a genuine improvement *as a design document*. It is a criticism of the *absence of the other kind of revision*: the kind that cuts a claim because a player didn't feel it. The GDD has never had that kind of revision. It needs one. The user's playtests are the source.

**Adjustment 19.** After the first gate run, revise the GDD by *removing* at least one player-experience claim that the gate did not support — not by adding a new framing that preserves it. A document that can only grow is a document that is not being tested. The discipline of *subtraction* is the antidote to the confirmation bias of *addition*.

## 20. Synthesis: the player-POV reconciliation of the two contentious views

The prior critique's finding #10 and this document's meta-attack (§0) name the same tension: **the GDD is a designer's document, sophisticated and self-consistent, and it has never met a player.** The two views:

- **Designer's view:** the design is coherent, the risks are named, the gate is well-formed, the precedents support the choices, and the build has proven the engineering.
- **Player's view:** the design is a hypothesis about my feelings; the gate is a designer's instrument measuring designer-chosen signals; the precedents are cited selectively; and the build has proven the engineering, not the experience.

**The reconciliation from the player's seat: both views are true, and the second does not negate the first.** A coherent, self-aware, well-precedented design is *the best possible starting position* for a playtest. It is not a substitute for one. The GDD's quality has raised the *prior probability* that the design works — but prior probability is not posterior evidence. The player-POV attack's contribution is to identify *which* claims the playtest must falsify or confirm, ranked by how confirmation-bias-prone they are:

1. **The §3a fun claim** (taming → optimising, same verb, parse-hinged) — most elegant, most unfalsifiable. Measure: unprompted specificity of "what I'd do differently."
2. **The §3b endogeneity claim** (escalation is self-generated) — true in the sim, authored in the experience. Measure: does the player attribute escalation to their own success or to the game?
3. **The §4.11 discovery claim** (capability discovered, not purchased) — unfalsifiable as stated. Measure: unprompted discovery vs. diagnostic-nudged "discovery."
4. **The §4.12 novice-floor claim** (parse is dark matter for the novice) — asserted against the genre grain. Measure: can a non-parse-opening tester reach functional?
5. **The §4.10 mastery-preservation claim** (the flip doesn't rug-pull mastery) — unaddressed by the four commitments. Measure: does a master carrier feel advantaged or reset at the flip?
6. **The §4.3a same-verb claim for topology** (Level 2 is the same verb at higher altitude) — unverifiable until M4. Measure: does the novice's verb (accept + watch) evolve continuously into the master's verb (author a graph), or is there a discontinuity?

These six are the confirmation-bias load-bearing claims. If the playtests confirm them, the GDD is a validated theory. If they refute any, the GDD must be revised by *subtraction* (Adjustment 19), not by *reframing* the refuted claim into a new form that preserves it.

## 21. What this document does NOT do (fences)

- **Does not run playtests.** The user handles empirical validation. This document designs the *measurements* the user should make; it does not make them.
- **Does not modify the GDD.** The GDD is the user's domain. All adjustments are proposals for the user to weigh.
- **Does not claim the design is bad.** It claims the design is a *strong, unvalidated hypothesis*, and identifies which hypotheses are most prone to surviving on confirmation bias alone.
- **Does not contest the engineering.** The sim/render split, the determinism, the f64 truth, the orrery, the WM — these are proven (per FINDINGS and the backlog) and are not attacked here.
- **Does not cite precedents the GDD didn't.** Every precedent verification here uses a precedent the GDD *already cited*, to test whether the GDD's *use* of it holds. External precedents are marked **[INFERENCE]** and are reasoning-by-analogy, not established theory.
